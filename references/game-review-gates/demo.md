# Demo

`Demo` means the game offers a meaningful slice with a visible beginning, middle, and end, or at least a bounded session that communicates the promise clearly. The game is no longer merely proving a mechanic. It is proving the product direction. However, scope, progression, depth, or polish are still limited enough that the build reads as a sample rather than a full release.

## What is usually true at this gate

- a new player can understand what kind of game this is
- there is enough content or structure to judge the promise
- the game can create a real first impression, not just a mechanical test
- some dimensions may already be strong, but the whole package is still narrow

## What is not yet true

- the game may not yet show durable retention or content breadth
- the build may still depend on future systems, balancing, or polish to feel fully release-ready
- the player may leave impressed, but not yet convinced of lasting value

## Comparison discipline

Compare against festival demos, Steam Next Fest style demos, vertical-slice presentations, and the early public samples of similar games. The bar here is communication and coherence.

Ask:

- does this demo prove the fantasy clearly?
- does it stop at the right time, or end before the player understands why the game matters?
- is it better or worse than strong demos in the same category at motivating wishlist or replay intent?

## Review expectations

- FTUE and listing honesty matter strongly because the player is judging the game's promise quickly
- gameplay must already feel coherent, even if not deep
- visuals should support a convincing identity, even if not final
- performance and stability should be good enough that the slice can be trusted

## What a good demo proves

A strong demo should prove at least three things:

- the player fantasy is understandable
- the quality direction is credible
- the creator knows what part of the game is strongest and has centered the slice around it

If the submission is playable but still fails to prove those points, it is probably a strong prototype rather than a true demo.

## Common misclassification risk

Do not call a build `Vertical Slice` just because the art looks better. A true vertical slice shows a representative quality target across the important systems, not just a better-looking demo.

## Feedback framing

At this gate, useful feedback helps the creator sharpen the slice:

- what part of the promise already lands
- where the slice feels thinner than the listing implies
- what one or two improvements would make the demo more convincing
- whether the demo is ending before the player receives the strongest proof of value

Good demo feedback should also call out what not to expand yet. Scope discipline matters here as much as polish.
