# Concept

`Concept` means the submission presents an idea, promise, or interaction direction, but it is not yet functioning as a real playable product. The player can see what the creator wants to build, but cannot yet experience a stable, honest loop that proves it.

This gate is not an insult. It is a diagnosis. Many early projects are at concept stage even if they already have some input, art, or menu work. The key question is not "is there content on screen." The key question is "can a player actually understand and evaluate the game through play?"

## What is usually true at this gate

- the core fantasy is visible mostly through intention rather than through completed interaction
- placeholder systems dominate the experience
- there may be only fragments of the loop
- menus or listing may exist before the runtime is meaningfully testable
- art, audio, and structure may imply a future game more than they support a current one

## What is not yet true

- the player cannot reliably complete a satisfying loop
- the game is not yet honest to market as a normal playable release
- comparison to finished products should be limited to direction, not to product quality

## Comparison discipline

When a game is at concept stage, compare it to early proofs from similar genres, not only to finished hits. Ask whether the current build has identified the right player promise and whether the first usable expression of that promise is emerging.

If the creator is clearly reaching toward a known reference space, note that. For example:

- "This wants to be a run-based action game, but the build is still proving movement and encounter identity."
- "This wants to be a cozy builder, but right now the interaction is closer to a placement mock-up than a self-sustaining play loop."

## Review expectations

You still score every dimension. The rubric does not disappear at early stages. What changes is how you interpret the result.

- gameplay should be judged on whether the loop exists at all
- controls should be judged on whether the intended interaction can be meaningfully tested
- visuals can be rough, but must not make the game unreadable if the creator expects feedback on play
- listing honesty matters a lot because over-positioning a concept build creates distrust

## Common misclassification risk

Do not call a build `Gameplay Prototype` just because some controls work. If a player still cannot meaningfully understand the loop or product promise through actual play, `Concept` is the more honest gate.

## Feedback framing

At this gate, the most useful guidance narrows focus:

- prove one loop
- prove one player fantasy
- remove decorative work that obscures the real missing piece
- align the listing with what is actually playable today

The creator should leave understanding what must become real next, not with a giant pile of polish chores.
