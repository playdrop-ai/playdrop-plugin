# Game Review Comparative Method

Use this method before scoring any dimension. The review standard is not "does this seem fine in isolation." The standard is "how does this compare to successful games that are genuinely close to it in player promise, platform, scope, and current stage."

The purpose is player trust. A generous review of a broken or prototype-grade game puts weak content in front of PlayDrop players, damages confidence in the platform, and makes future discovery worse. Do not inflate scores to be encouraging. Use the benchmark to decide what a real player would accept from the competition today.

This is how experienced game teams and serious reviewers avoid vague feedback. They benchmark. They compare. They name the quality bar. They decide whether the current build is below, at, or above that bar for the stage it is in.

## 1. Choose the right comparison set

Pick two to four reference games before writing scores.

Good comparison candidates are:

- same genre or subgenre
- same primary control scheme
- same player fantasy
- same session length or pacing
- same surface constraints such as desktop, mobile landscape, portrait, controller-heavy, or touch-heavy
- same maturity when possible, such as prototype against prototype footage, demo against festival demo, early public release against early public release

Do not compare a rough prototype to the fully mature version of an unrelated blockbuster and call the result useful. If a direct same-stage comparison is not available, compare to a shipped peer but explicitly adjust expectations for stage.

## 2. Compare promises, not just mechanics

The right reference is not always the game with the most similar mechanics. It is often the game making the most similar promise to the player.

Examples:

- A short score-chasing survival game may compare better to `Vampire Survivors`, `Brotato`, or `Nova Drift` than to a traditional action RPG.
- A tactile precision platformer may compare better to `Celeste`, `Super Meat Boy`, or `N++` than to a broader action-adventure game.
- A cozy build-and-optimize loop may compare better to `Mini Motorways`, `Dorfromantik`, or `Mini Metro` than to a city sim with much larger scale.

Ask: when a player sees the listing, what successful game will they subconsciously compare this to?

If the submitted game is a clone or mini version of a known genre, compare it against the closest successful examples of that genre's player fantasy, not against the easiest possible interpretation of its implementation.

## 3. Decide the bar for the current stage

The review is stage-aware, but stage-aware does not mean permissive.

- A `Gameplay Prototype` can be rough in art and content breadth, but the core interaction still needs to be readable and testable.
- A `Demo` can be short, but it must communicate the promise clearly.
- A `First Release` does not need feature-complete depth, but it does need honest listing, stable runtime, and a usable first session.
- A `Mature Live Version` is judged against stronger expectations for retention, clarity, and consistency.

For every comparison, ask:

- What would a player reasonably forgive at this stage?
- What would immediately make them distrust, bounce, or disengage?
- What is the smallest set of qualities that must already feel real?

## 4. Judge each dimension against the comparison set

For every dimension, answer:

- Which reference games are most relevant here?
- Is this submission clearly worse, roughly comparable, or clearly stronger on this dimension?
- Is the weakness structural, or just a matter of content depth or polish?
- Is the current build failing the bar for its stage, or simply not yet best-in-class?
- Would this dimension make a new PlayDrop player trust the platform more or less?

Do not use comparison as a shortcut to taste. Use it to anchor the quality bar and make the review more concrete.

## 5. Turn comparisons into actionable feedback

The point of benchmarking is not to say "be more like Game X." The point is to say what quality pattern works elsewhere and how this game falls short of it.

Good feedback sounds like:

- "The restart-to-action time is much slower than the best score-attack games in this space. Tightening the reset loop would increase replayability immediately."
- "The game wants the readability of a `Slay the Spire` or `Balatro` style decision screen, but the current card text hierarchy is much harder to parse under pressure."
- "The jump and air-control feel is below the baseline players expect from precision platformers. The problem is not difficulty, it is unreliable response."

Bad feedback sounds like:

- "Needs more polish."
- "Does not feel as good as popular games."
- "Graphics should be better."

## 6. Keep comparisons honest

Do not force named references if they are not useful. Sometimes the best benchmark is a category:

- top-down extraction shooters
- one-button endless runners
- mobile merge games
- arcade drift racers

When using named references, pick games that the creator would recognize as legitimate quality signals, not obscure personal favorites.

## 7. Keep the review grounded in evidence

Benchmarking is only useful if it is tied to observed play.

- Launch the game.
- Play enough to observe the loop.
- Test the declared input path.
- Compare listing claims to the real runtime.
- Note the exact friction points.

Then score. Never reverse that order.
