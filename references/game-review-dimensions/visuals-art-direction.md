# Visuals / Art Direction

This dimension is not a beauty contest. It is about whether the visual layer supports the game honestly and effectively. Strong art direction can exist at low fidelity if it is coherent. Weak art direction can exist at high fidelity if the game is visually confused, unreadable, or tonally inconsistent.

## Comparison discipline

Compare against games with similar camera, asset complexity, and readability demands.

- minimalist games should be compared against the clarity and intentionality of strong minimalist references
- stylized 3D games should be compared on shape language, readability, and cohesion, not just shader count
- UI-heavy games should be compared on information design as much as pure asset quality

The key question is whether the current visual language helps the player play and trust the game, relative to relevant peers.

## What to inspect

- silhouette clarity
- contrast and visual hierarchy
- consistency between environments, characters, effects, and UI
- tone and theme coherence
- readability under motion and pressure
- whether the listing visuals represent the runtime honestly
- whether the runtime is production-grade or prototype-grade relative to the player promise
- whether camera and perspective help the player read the core action

## Evidence to gather

- inspect the game in motion, not just in paused screenshots
- look for moments where players may misread threat, affordance, or objective
- note whether roughness is acceptable stage roughness or whether it signals missing direction
- compare the runtime look with screenshots, cover art, and iconography
- place captured gameplay screenshots beside the closest references when the visual promise is in dispute

## Questions to answer

- Can the player instantly tell what matters on screen?
- Does the game have an intentional look, even if it uses placeholder or limited assets?
- Are style choices helping gameplay or fighting it?
- Does the UI belong to the same product as the gameplay visuals?
- Would the submitted runtime look credible beside the selected reference set in a store, social preview, or creator portfolio?
- Is roughness an intentional visual language, or does it read as placeholder implementation?

## Common failure modes

- high-detail backgrounds that bury gameplay information
- mismatched UI and world-art tone
- placeholder assets mixed with final-looking assets in a way that destroys coherence
- effects that feel exciting in isolation but reduce clarity in actual play
- screenshots or icons promising a stronger visual product than the runtime delivers
- production-grade references paired with prototype-grade runtime visuals
- camera or perspective choices that make the core action visually misleading

## Stage-aware interpretation

At `Gameplay Prototype`, temporary art is acceptable if readability is preserved and direction is visible. At `Vertical Slice`, direction should feel intentional and persuasive. By `Mature Live Version`, visual inconsistency or unreadability becomes a real product quality problem.

Do not give production-grade visual scores to prototype-grade substitutes. If a game promises the appeal of polished 3D mobile references but ships sparse abstract placeholder-like scenes, cap visuals at `4/10`. If the runtime would look embarrassing beside the selected references, cap visuals at `5/10` even when the palette is coherent.

## What strong feedback looks like

- "The art is low-fidelity but coherent, which is acceptable for this stage. The larger issue is that enemy bullets and pickups share too much visual weight, so the game reads worse than its arcade references."
- "The listing promises a polished neon action game, but the runtime currently looks much earlier-stage than that. The problem is trust, not just polish."
